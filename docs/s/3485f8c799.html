Massive Czech Protests Over Cuts to Public Broadcasting

Politics📍 Letná Plain, Prague, Czechia 🌍 · 2026-11-15 · Newser

📌 What happened

More than 350,000 Czechs rallied in Prague on Sunday against government plans to scrap the licence fees that fund public broadcasters. Organisers called it one of the biggest protests in the country's history. Protesters say the plan would give ministers more control over newsrooms and cut broadcaster budgets by about 15%.

📖 The full story

Organisers said more than 350,000 people joined the Sunday rally in Prague, one of the largest in Czech history, to defend the mandatory licence fees that pay for Czechia's public broadcasters. It took place at Letná Plain, the cradle of the 1989 Velvet Revolution that ended more than 40 years of communist rule. Many wore triangular paper hats like Večerníček, a children's bedtime TV mascot that became the protest's symbol, and banners said the media would not be given away.

The plan would end the licence fees, which together add up to 205 koruna (£7) a month, and pay for broadcasters from the state budget instead. Protesters say that would hand ministers more leverage over newsrooms, and the changes would cut state television and radio budgets by about 15%. The bill's first reading was pushed through in the middle of the night in late October, and since then there have been repeated protests, weeks of demonstrations in towns and cities, and a strike by Czech TV and Czech Radio staff.

The Million Moments for Democracy group organised the rally and demanded the government drop the plan, warning that Babiš and his coalition partners threaten democracy. Lead organiser Mikuláš Minář said the rally was extraordinary and only the beginning. Singer Šárka Dumišincová said the media meant freedom to her, while computer programmer Jan Snasel said the government was trying to control the media, cut their funding and influence what they broadcast; banners rejected the Slovak scenario and told the government to keep its hands off the media.

The European Broadcasting Union, the European Federation of Journalists, Reporters Without Borders and the International Press Institute also criticised the plan, comparing it to steps by Slovakia's prime minister Robert Fico and Hungary's former premier Viktor Orbán; protesters cited Hungary, Poland, Serbia and Slovakia as places where rightwing populist governments weakened media independence. Babiš, a billionaire who returned to power last year, said protesters were stirring up the public with unfounded claims, wrote an op-ed about the opposition's seven lies over the fees, and said anyone talking about nationalisation was lying deliberately and that the government would never interfere in editorial work. His coalition includes his ANO movement, the rightwing Motorists for Themselves and the anti-migrant, pro-Russian Freedom and Direct Democracy party, and ANO still has about 30% support in opinion polls.
