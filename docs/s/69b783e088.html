📖 The full story

Donald Trump, the United States president, will attend the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in Shenzhen, China, this week, where 21 Pacific Rim member economies are taking part. He is expected to meet Chinese President Xi Jinping and to hold a three-way meeting with Xi and Russian President Vladimir Putin. A Trump-Xi meeting on the sidelines would be the third time the two leaders have met this year, after Xi hosted Trump in Beijing in May and Trump hosted Xi at the White House in September, with earlier meetings heavy on ceremony, aimed at showing stability in a consequential relationship, and light on meaningful breakthroughs.

Experts predicted a similar approach in Shenzhen, with AI regulation, trade and the self-ruling island of Taiwan likely to loom large. The summit comes just days after Democrats won commanding control of both chambers of the US Congress, a shift that will likely serve as a major check on Trump in his final two years, while Beijing is increasingly looking beyond the Trump era to see what comes next. Ali Wyne, a senior research and advocacy adviser on US-China relations at Crisis Group, said Trump will work to strengthen his rapport with Xi but the meeting is unlikely to produce substantive breakthroughs, Yury Ushakov, Putin's foreign policy adviser, told reporters on Monday that the significance and symbolism of the meetings between the three superpower leaders are hard to overestimate, neither side has released a formal agenda for the expected Trump-Xi meeting, and the White House said the countries will support each other at APEC and at the G20 summit in Florida in December and agreed to build a constructive relationship of strategic stability based on respect, fairness and reciprocity.

AI regulation is set to loom large, with the US and China, as industry leaders, facing pressure to jointly agree safeguards on the transformative technology, after both sides pledged in September to set up a mechanism to quickly communicate AI-related incidents and hold a dialogue on emerging risks. The Trump administration has repeatedly downplayed the need to integrate developmental guardrails, Trump said after the White House meeting that the US would not be putting on brakes on the AI industry, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, speaking at a National Committee on US-China Relations event last week, said both sides expect to exchange best practices for evaluating and testing advanced models before their release and for keeping human judgment firmly in command of this technology, while adding that they should harbour no illusions about the differences between the United States and China and that their approach to every challenge will not always converge. The recent resignation of Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, which followed revelations that he had an affair with an alleged Chinese spy, will also loom large over any Trump-Xi meeting even if it is not directly addressed, because Huang's close relationship with Trump, including accompanying the US president to Beijing during his May visit, and his ties to alleged spy Alice Huang have renewed questions about the influence he exerted over the US government as he pushed to ease restrictions on the sale of advanced AI chips to Beijing; Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick has faced particular scrutiny, including from prominent members of the Republican Party, over his reportedly lax enforcement of export controls related to China, and Crisis Group's Wyne said Trump will be under enormous pressure from Congress and members of his own administration to raise the scandal and warn Xi that their relationship could suffer if Trump concludes that Xi has been deploying Huang and other prominent business executives to sabotage US artificial intelligence efforts; some experts expect it to have little bearing on his interaction with his Chinese counterpart, and Gabriel Wildau, managing director at the advisory firm Teneo, told Al Jazeera last week that Trump shows no sign that he intends to disrupt the current US-China rapprochement; the Reuters news agency reported on Friday that US officials said Trump is not expected to raise the issue of Alice Huang with Xi, and China has denied Alice Huang was working as a spy on its behalf.

Trump has already bucked longstanding US policy on Taiwan during his recent meeting with Xi. Following the Beijing summit in May, Trump told reporters he had discussed US arms sales to the self-governing island, which Beijing claims as its own, in great detail with the Chinese president, even though the US has provided arms to Taiwan since 1979 as mandated under the Taiwan Relations Act and, under the 1982 Six Assurances document, Washington has not consulted with China on such sales. In a subsequent Fox News interview, Trump referred to Taiwan as a very good negotiating chip with China and has since held up approval of a $14bn arms package earmarked for the island, while Bonnie Glaser and Noah Munk of the German Marshall Fund of the United States (GMF) wrote that the language invites Beijing to expect something in return for each future approval and that the more successful the bargaining appears, the more Beijing will press it and treat the next notification as a concession from Washington rather than a routine measure under the Taiwan Relations Act; the White House defended the delay by citing limited production capacity, particularly amid the US-Israel war with Iran, and the delicate nature of the US-China trade truce, and Democratic control of Congress plus general bipartisan support for Taiwan could see pressure increase on the issue in Trump's final two years.
